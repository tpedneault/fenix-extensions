Install

  1. In Fenix, press SPC h x to open the Extensions page.
  2. Search for NATS, and install it. Fenix shows what it may do before it installs anything.

Or download the package and run fenix --install-extension fenix.nats-1.2.0.fenixext.

What it may do

Fenix holds it to these, and shows them before installing it. Everything else is closed to it.

Opens network connections (TCP and UDP sockets) to these addresses
  • ${setting:nats.servers} those the setting nats.servers holds
  • *:4222 any host, this port
Keeps files in folders of yours
  • creds: the folder the setting nats.creds_folder names, else nats-creds in Fenix's data folder
  • exports: the folder the setting nats.exports names, else nats-exports in Fenix's data folder
Reads and keeps secrets in Fenix's secret store, under these keys
  • nats.auth.*

About

A NATS client in Fenix: what's on your servers, live, a section a subject -- how many, how fast, how big, the last minute's rate drawn -- opened to its messages, newest first, and a message opened to its headers and its payload, JSON pretty-printed. Publish and request from a buffer, send a message again or edit it first, and browse JetStream's streams, consumers and key-value buckets. It keeps up with a busy server: at hundreds of thousands of messages a second Fenix stays as quick as it was, and the server never sees a slow consumer.

SPC o m m opens it and connects to the servers in nats.servers.

[nats]
servers = ["nats://localhost:4222", "tls://prod.example.com:4443"]
subjects = [">"]

  • A subject is a row: its messages, its rate a second (the last three seconds'), when it was last seen, its average size and a sparkline of the last 30 seconds. Enter opens it to its messages, newest first: the time, the size, how many headers, the reply subject and the start of the payload. Enter on a message opens it: its headers, then its payload -- JSON indented and coloured, text as it is, anything else as a hex dump. o opens it whole in a buffer.

  • The focus stays put. New messages come in above the one you're on; it stays where it is on screen, and so does the message.

  • A tree (t): subjects grouped by their tokens, telemetry.* with its subjects' totals, opened a token at a time.

  • Sorted (s) by name, rate, count or when last seen.

  • Subscriptions: a subscribes to a subject (* a token, > the rest), d unsubscribes, live, on every server; they're nats.subjects.

  • Pause (P): nothing new is stored, so what you're reading stays; counting goes on.

  • Several servers: each of nats.servers is a connection of its own; c shows the next one's subjects, C connects or disconnects the one shown.

Filters

  • / finds words in subjects and payloads, as you type.

  • f writes a filter: each word a condition, all of which hold.

    WordHolds when
    orders.>, subject:orders.*.euthe subject matches (wildcards)
    h:Nats-Msg-Id, header:region=euit has the header, with that value
    re:"time(out)?"its payload matches the regular expression
    json:.status=failed, json:$.items[0].qty=2its payload is JSON with that value there
    size:100..2k, size:>64kits size, headers and payload
    any other wordit's in the subject or the payload, any case

    The filter's subjects become the subscriptions on the server: with orders.* in the filter, a > subscription is narrowed to orders.*, so what you'd hide isn't even sent. Its line under the subscriptions says so.

  • F keeps filters by name, and uses them again.

  • H writes highlight rules: what a rule matches is coloured, not hidden. A rule starting with ! also notifies, once a rule every 30 seconds at most, with how many matched meanwhile.

    warn  orders.* json:.status=failed
!bad  re:panic|fatal
#4a90d9 h:Nats-Msg-Id size:>64k

Keeping up

The extension runs on a thread of its own, and Fenix lays a page out between its calls; a socket is read on another thread, 64 KiB at a time, and stops being read when 8 MiB wait for the extension -- at which point the server would see a slow consumer, and cut it off. So every message costs as little as it can:

  • Each message is counted and added to its subject's rate: a few additions. Counts are exact, always.

  • Its bytes are copied once, as they are, into blocks of 1 MiB that are reused: no allocation per message. They're only read when a row shows them, and only decoded when you open one.

  • Memory is a budget (nats.budget_mib, 64 MiB): every subject of every server together; the oldest messages go first when it's spent. A subject also keeps at most nats.per_subject messages and nats.per_subject_kib.

  • The page is drawn on a timer (nats.refresh_hz, 10 a second), only when something changed and it's on screen -- never for a message -- and only the rows on screen are laid out.

  • Filters that read payloads (a word, a regular expression, a JSON path, a header) read them on the subjects you're looking at, a few milliseconds a frame, newest first, and remember what they found; the subject and the size are checked from what's kept of every message without reading it.

  • It watches its own load: the share of its time spent on what came, and how late its own timer is (a timer behind a queue of data comes as late as the queue). When either is high it stores 1 message in N of a busy subject's (sampling 1:2, 1:4, ... on the page, with how many weren't stored) while a quiet subject (under 20 a second) keeps every message, and it goes back down when it's been calm for three seconds. At the extreme it only counts.

On a laptop-class machine the path from socket to store takes about 3 million messages a second of 16 B to 1 KiB, 5 million of 16 to 64 B, natively, 99% of 64 KiB chunks in under 0.3 ms and the longest a few milliseconds, one in thousands (tests/throughput.rs). Inside Fenix, as WebAssembly, a local server sending as fast as it could was taken at 590,000 messages a second, busy 40% of the time, the page as quick to answer as an idle one.

Publishing

p (or SPC o m p) opens a buffer to write a message in, as JSON:

{
  "subject": "orders.created",
  "server": "localhost:4222",
  "headers": { "Nats-Msg-Id": "42" },
  "reply": "",
  "payload": { "order": 42 }
}

A string payload is sent as its text, any other JSON as compact JSON, payload_base64 as bytes. SPC o m s sends it; SPC o m r (or r on the page) sends it as a request, and the reply opens beside, with how long it took -- or says no one answers.

The server can only say no one answers when nothing is subscribed to the subject, and a > subscription is interested in everything. So with the default nats.subjects, a request no one answers waits for its timeout, and that goes for every client of the server while Fenix is connected. Fenix says so once on a server that isn't this machine. On a shared server, narrow nats.subjects, or a filter, to the subjects you want.

On a message: R sends it again as it was, e opens it in the buffer to change first, y copies its payload, Y copies it as a nats pub command line, headers and all. x writes a subject's stored messages to a JSONL file, X every message the filter lets through (nats.exports).

JetStream

J (or SPC o m j): the account's streams and key-value buckets.

  • A stream opens to its configuration, its sequence range and subjects, its consumers -- pending, waiting for an ack, redelivered, push or pull -- and its messages: b reads them from the first, n the next page, g from a sequence, t from a time (10:00, 2024-05-01 10:00, 15m ago; NATS 2.11). P purges it (you type its name), d deletes the message you're on.

  • A bucket opens to its keys; a key to its value and its history. p puts a value, d deletes a key, w watches the bucket's changes as they come.

Credentials

SPC o m a sets how a server is signed in to: a user and password, a token, an NKey seed (signing the server's nonce), or a .creds file (a JWT and its seed, read from nats.creds_folder). They're kept in the system's keychain (nats.auth.<host:port>), never in the URL or the settings. A sign-in the server refuses is said once, and tried again every 30 seconds until the credentials change.

Connections are dialled again when they drop, after 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 15, then every 30 seconds, and their subscriptions made again. A PING every 20 seconds keeps each alive and measures its round trip; three unanswered and it's taken for dead. A server that wants TLS on a nats:// URL gets it after its INFO; tls:// is TLS from the start. The modeline says how they are: NATS ● up 2.0k/s.

Limits

  • A message keeps at most its first 1 MiB; it says so when it's cut. One bigger than 64 MiB (more than a server's max_payload can be) ends the connection.

  • Times are kept to the millisecond for 49 days after Fenix starts.

  • The extension's memory is capped at 128 MiB, so the budget at 80.

  • connect_urls (a cluster's other servers) aren't followed: each server is one you list, which is what its permission lets it reach.

  • Reading a stream by time asks JetStream for start_time: NATS 2.11.

  • Its own replies are left out of the live view; everyone else's _INBOX traffic a > sees is shown, as it is.

What it may do

  • Open sockets to the servers in nats.servers, and to any host on 4222, for one whose port is left out.

  • Read and keep secrets nats.auth.*, a server's credentials each, in the system's keychain.

  • Write exports to its exports folder, and read .creds files from its credentials folder.

  • Notify (API 1.3), when a rule marked ! matches.

Building

cargo xtask pack extensions/nats dist
cargo test --manifest-path extensions/nats/Cargo.toml
cargo test --release --manifest-path extensions/nats/Cargo.toml --test throughput -- --ignored --nocapture
cargo test --manifest-path crates/nats/Cargo.toml

The protocol is crates/nats (fenix-nats): the parser, read with the stream cut at every byte and every pair of bytes; CONNECT, SUB, PUB and HPUB; headers; subjects and wildcards; NKeys against RFC 8032's Ed25519 vector and .creds files; inboxes; JetStream's and KV's API. The extension's tests drive its connections, store, filters and pages with a fake network; the throughput test feeds a million synthetic messages through the same path.
